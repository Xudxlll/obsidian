# Market Rule & Assumption

# Short Sale: Definition

# Short Sale

A short sale is the act of borrowing shares and immediately selling them in the market, with the obligation to buy back the same number of shares in the future and return them to the lender. Also known as short selling or simply shorting, this strategy profits when the price of the security declines, and incurs a loss if the price rises.

# Portfolio: Definition & Value

# Portfolio

A portfolio is any (static or dynamic) collection of financial assets, such as stocks, bonds, and cash, held in long or short positions.

# Value of a Portfolio

The value of a portfolio is the amount of money required to transfer its ownership to a third party. This value can be positive, negative, or zero.

# What is Arbitrage?

# Arbitrage Opportunity

An arbitrage opportunity is a trading strategy on some [t, T ] that:

• Requires zero initial investment: $\Pi ( t ) = 0 ;$ ;   
• Guarantees non-negative payoff: $\Pi ( T ) \geqslant 0 ;$   
Has a strictly positive probability of gain: $\mathbb { P } ( \Pi ( T ) > 0 ) > 0 ;$ where ⇧(s) is the portfolio value at time s.

# Key Market Assumption

# Arbitrage-Free Market

There are no arbitrage opportunities in the financial market. If one arises, rational investors will exploit it, and competition will quickly eliminate the opportunity.

# Well-Informed Investors

All investors have immediate access to market information. As soon as an arbitrage opportunity appears, it is known and acted upon by all, causing it to disappear almost instantaneously.

# Frictionless Market & No Bid-Ask Spread

# Frictionless Market

There are no transaction costs or taxes in trading. This assumption simplifies the mathematics and is a common starting point in financial modeling.

# No Bid-Ask Spread

At any moment, the buying (bid) and selling (ask) prices of each asset are identical. In practice, the ask price is usually higher than the bid price, but we assume equality for theoretical analysis.

# Remark

The above assumptions are idealizations. As technology and market infrastructure improve (e.g., faster computers, better communication), these assumptions become increasingly realistic.   
This course emphasizes two key financial concepts that have revolutionized derivative pricing: replication of payoffs and the absence of arbitrage.   
We will explore mathematical models that describe how asset prices behave under these assumptions, providing powerful tools for pricing derivatives. Details and examples will be introduced throughout the course.
