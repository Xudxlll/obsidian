7.1 Forward and Futures
Definition of Forward Contract
It is an agreement between two parties, a buyer and a seller, that calls for the delivery of an asset at a future point in time with a price agreed upon today.
Definition of Futures Contract
It is a forward contract that has standardized terms, is traded on an organized exchange, and follows a daily settlement procedure in which the losses of one party to the contract are paid to the other party.

## Slide 13

Organized Futures Trading
Delivery Terms
Delivery Date(s)
Most contracts allow delivery on any day of the month following a particular day, usually the first business day of the month.
Delivery Procedure
For stock index futures and other cash-settled contracts, the settlement occurs on the last trading day or on the day after the last trading day.
For non-cash-settled contracts, the delivery procedure must be specified.
E.g. the deliverable spot commodity must be sent to any of several eligible locations.
Financial adjustments to the price received upon delivery are required when an acceptable but lower-grade commodity is delivered.
Expiration Months
E.g. harvestable commodities usually have expiration months corresponding with harvest months.
Financial futures typically have expirations in every quarter.
Final Trading Day
Given an expiration month, the final trading days are typically the 3rd Friday of the month and the business day prior to the last business day of the month.

## Slide 17

The Mechanics of Futures Trading
Margin Accounts
“Margin” or performance bond: the margin deposit for futures trading is more like a good-faith security.
This margin is not the same as the margin account in stock trading, which is essentially borrowing stocks from the brokerage firm.
Initial margin:  an amount that must be deposited on the day the transaction is opened
Maintenance margin: the amount that must be maintained every day thereafter.
Variation margin: the additional funds deposited due to margin call.
Settlement
Settlement price: average of the prices of the last few trades of the day.  Each account is marked to market.
Daily settlement: if the difference between current and previous settlement price is +, then the dollar amount is credited to the margins accounts of those holding long positions and charged to the accounts holding short positions.
Open Interest
The total number of futures contracts outstanding at any one time.

## Slide 18

The Mechanics of Futures Trading
Physical Delivery
Three-day delivery process
The clearing member firms report to the clearinghouse those of their customers who hold long positions.
Position Day - two business days before the intended delivery day, the holder of a short position who intends to make delivery notifies the clearinghouse of its desire to deliver.
Notice of intention day - the exchange selects the holder of the oldest long position to receive delivery.
Delivery day - delivery takes place and the long pays the short.
Deliverable instrument
Most futures contracts allow for more than one deliverable instrument.
Specified price adjustment to reflect a difference in the quality of the deliverable good.
Cash Settlement
The settlement price on the last trading day is fixed at the closing spot price of the underlying instrument.
All contracts are marked to market on that day, and the positions are deemed to be closed.

## Slide 19

The Mechanics of Futures Trading
Offsetting
About 99% of all futures contracts are not delivered or cash settled.
A process in which the traders close out their positions prior to expiration.
Exchange for Physicals (EFP)
Some contracts (such as oil futures) are settled known as delivery against actuals or delivery versus cash.
The only type of futures occurs off the floor of the exchange but needs to report the transaction to the CME Group as though each had offset its contract with each other.
The EFP market gives the parties additional flexibility in making delivery, choosing the terms, and conducting such business when the exchanges are closed.
Forward Contracts
They do not have standard terms and conditions like futures contracts; it is not as simple to offset them.  Hence, when a forward contract is written, the intention is generally to hold it to the expiration day and take delivery.

## Slide 24

Pricing of Futures
The Cost-of-Carry Model (CCM)
The general continuous-time version:
Fte-rT = Ste-yT
Futures on assets such as stock index that makes a continuous dividend payment, y = d.
Futures on foreign currencies earning foreign-currency interest, y = r*.
Futures on commodities with convenience yield, y, such as cotton.
Futures on commodities with storage costs, -y, such as gold and silver.
The special discrete-time version:
Futures price = Spot price + Interest cost + Storage Cost
Since the underlying portfolio of stocks pays dividends, storage cost is a gain:	Futures price = Spot price + Interest cost - Reinvested dividend
Case I:  The dividends are paid at irregular dates prior to future expiration date:
Futures price = Cash index + Interest cost -  (FV(Divs))
where   (FV(Divs)) = future value of all dividends paid by the component stocks of the index prior to the expiration date
Case II:  The dividends are paid at a annual constant rate d:
Futures price = Cash index ( 1 + (r - d)(T/365))
where  r = annual interest rate,  T = time to expiration

## Slide 25

Pricing of Futures
Example:  Interest Rate Parity in the Currency Market
Fte-rT = Ste-yT = Ste-r*T.
Futures on foreign currencies earning foreign-currency interest, y = r*.
A common, discrete-time version:
Fd/f x (1 + rf) = Sd/f x (1 + rd).
(1 + rf) = Sd/f x (1 + rd) x Ff/d.
In log form, Fd/f – Sd/f = rd – rf.
Forward discount equals to the interest rate differential.
It is called interest-rate parity in international finance.

Basis, Contango, and Backwardation
Basis  Current spot price – Futures price
When basis (positive) turns wide, basis is said to be strengthening.
When basis (positive) turns narrow, basis is said to be weakening.
Contango occurs when the basis is negative.
For storable assets, as well as for securities that do not pay interest or dividends, the cost of carry normally is positive.  This would cause the futures price to lie above the current spot price.

## Slide 31

Hedging with Futures
Hedge Risk
Principle (or Quantity) Risk
Basis risk
Recall that Basis  S – F.
Using futures to hedge the cash exposure can hence be interpreted as replacing the (cash) price risk to basis risk.
In general, basis risk increases as the time difference between the hedge expiration and the delivery month increases.
No direct hedge
If the asset you are holding does not have corresponding futures contract to hedge, then direct hedge is not possible.
Cross hedging is the use of futures contracts whose underlying assets are different from the assets you want to hedge but they are correlated in price movements.
All in all, unlike using forward contracts, using futures contracts can rarely achieve perfect hedge situation.

Option terminologies

Call vs. Put
Call – an option to buy an asset, a currency, or futures
Put – an option to sell an asset, a currency, or futures

The price at which an option permits its owner to buy or sell the underlying security, futures or currency

The date after which a derivative contract no longer exists
Moneyness
In-the-money (ITM):  strike price better than market price
At-the-money (ATM):  strike price = market price
Out-of-money (OTM):  strike price worse than market price

## Slide 52

Option-embedded Products
Convertible Bonds
Conversion
e.g. A convertible bond that is convertible into 40 shares (conversion price of $25) of the company stocks (trading at $20 per share) is trading at $950.
Conversion ratio
It is the number of shares for which each bond may be exchanged, which is the par value of the bond divided by the conversion price.
In the example, it is equal to 40.
Market conversion value
It is the conversion ratio times the current price per share of the stock.
In the example, it is equal to $20 x 40 = $800.
Conversion premium
It is the difference between the market price of the bond and the conversion value of the bond.
In the example it is equal to $950 – $800 = $150.
Convertible bonds yield less than non-convertible bonds of equivalent risk.
Convertible bonds are often call "sweeteners"; that is, the conversion option is offered to entice the investor into purchasing the bond.

## Slide 54

Option-embedded Products
Levered firm as options
From the shareholders’ point of view
Holding a call option (written by the bondholders) on their firm with an exercise price equal to the value of the debt (from the bondholders).
Simultaneously holding the firm’s stocks, selling short the debt (to the bondholders), and holding a put option (written by the bondholders) with an exercise price equal to the value of the debt.
Recall the put-call parity: C = S + P - PV(X).
From the bondholders’ point of view
Owning the firm and writing a call option (to the shareholders).
Simultaneously holding the bonds and writing a put option (to the shareholders) with an exercise price equal to the value of the bond.
Recall the put-call parity: S - C = PV(X) – P.
Hence, shareholders have an incentive to take high-risk projects.
